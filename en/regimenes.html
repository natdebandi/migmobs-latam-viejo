Free movement regimes

In collaboration with Freemove, the project led by Diego Acosta (University of Bristol) and Luuk van der Baaren that maps, analyses and compares bilateral and multilateral free movement of people regimes, with an interactive atlas at freemovehub.com. The dataset is introduced in Acosta and van der Baaren (2026), International Migration (in press). The corridor-year dummies used on this page are a coding by Tobias Grohmann based on Freemove.

The regimes

Every corridor, year by year

Each row is an origin → destination corridor, grouped by destination country; each column, a year. Colour shows the regime in force; overlaps have their own colour. ✕ marks corridors that lose the regime before 2022.

Reading. Of the 132 corridors among the 12 South American countries, 71 had some free movement regime in some year between 1995 and 2022 and 61 never did. The grey, which fills the years with no agreement, is the system's default state: free movement is the exception, not the rule.

Source. Grohmann, T. Coding of free movement regimes by corridor and year (one dummy per regime, from the destination country's perspective), based on Freemove. MIGMOBS project.

Flows and free movement

Migration and mobility to each country, with its regimes

Flows into the selected country, summed over all origins in the system. Vertical lines: first year that destination applies each regime to any corridor. Migration is on the left axis and mobility on the right: the two series do not share a scale, so levels are not comparable, only shapes.

Reading. Argentina has applied free movement regimes since 1999: AR (1999), MF+MA (2009). Between 1998 and 1999, migration to that country went from 44,430 to 47,930 people (index 1.08). This is temporal coincidence, not an estimated effect: there is no comparison group.

Reading. Bolivia has applied free movement regimes since 1999: AR (1999), C545 (2003), MA (2009), C878 (2021). Between 1998 and 1999, migration to that country went from 6,679 to 7,807 people (index 1.17). This is temporal coincidence, not an estimated effect: there is no comparison group.

Flows through corridors with and without free movement

Annual sum of flows among the 13 countries, by whether the corridor had a regime in force that year. Mobility: only corridors with data. Grey band: 2022–2023, data that may be distorted by the Gaskin and Abel model adjustment.

Reading. In 2019, 24% of migration between South American countries went through corridors with free movement (13% in 2000). 96% of that year's flow without a regime had its origin or destination in Venezuela, which is not part of the extended MERCOSUR in this coding.

Reading. In 2019, 95% of visitor trips between South American countries went through corridors with free movement (4% in 2000). 48% of that year's flow without a regime had its origin or destination in Venezuela, which is not part of the extended MERCOSUR in this coding.

Before and after entry into force

For each corridor that gains free movement between 1997 and 2017, each year's flow divided by the flow in the year before entry into force (=100); median across corridors. Descriptive only: no comparison group or controls.

Reading. Five years after entry into force, migration in the median of the 71 corridors was 123 (previous year = 100); mobility, 156. Both were already growing: five years before they stood at 85 and 74.
